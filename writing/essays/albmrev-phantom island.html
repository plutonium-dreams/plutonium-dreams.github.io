Album Review : Phantom Island (2025) by King Gizzard & The Lizzard Wizard

by Kent Nico L. Balondro

7/7/2025


Not a lot of music ring through my ears as though such notes satisfyingly strike the hammer, anvil, and stirrup. Loud, bombastic, and cheesy, Phantom Island evokes the feeling of an era bygone with the sound of horns and strings coupled with masterful orchestration.

The first few seconds of the record greets the listener with the eponymous track appearing to be a sort of thriller-esque, brought by the arpeggio of an inquisitive chord and the calls of strings and woodwinds interspaced by the low roar of brass - think big band. It comes as a surprise that this preoverture builds up to a sound more akin to soul music (harmonized vocals like that of the Bee Gees) and jazz funk touched with a hint of cheesy rock and roll. The preamble has set the grandiose, sonic mood for the show.

The album truly begins with the second track, Dead Stick. A build up reminiscent of Tom and Jerry gives the entrance to a goofy ahh track. The inspirations of 1950s rock and roll - the likes of Elvis Presley - can be heard with the variations of lively twelve-bar-blues; the big vibe in particular gives off the feeling of walking in a celebrated city greeting lively people one comes across on the street. In an artistically connected way, the bridge of this track can be easily matched with the chorus of September by Earth, Wind, and Fire. The lines "Deadstick, death wish, panic in the cockpit" and "Badeya, say do you remember" have almost the same harmony and rhythm that one can splice both and still form a melodically-comprehensible song. This connection can also be made with the lines of "Mayday! Mayday! Mayday! Save me!" being answered by "- It's a rich man's world" from ABBA's Money, Money, Money. Perhaps it might be a bit of a stretch for me to make these links, however I find it interesting that many of the sounds in music - not just the ones present in this album - are filled with inspiration from the records of past.

This bombastic tune is proceeded by a somber song: Lonely Cosmos. Evoking the feeling of solitary space travel - explored by the likes of David Bowie in the 70s - through the rather cliché story of a homesick spaceman. The instrumental sections make free-fall with hope and the country-soul bars compose playfulness in a rather depressing place. Imagine playing this song while homesickly dreading the distance from home, what a contrast of emotions that would be. The song ends with strings swinging about and descending and one could feel as though they are far too distant from what they are longing.

The down feeling from the previous track is risen by the early-morning-hope elicited by Eternal Return. The Beatles influence can be strongly heard with the chorus and familiar violins. Onwards, a feel-good soul section and traces of acapella paint a goofy vibe. These perky and abrupt staccatos are contrasted with the slow moving chorus. The bridge in particular reminds me of Pharell Williams for its sweet soulful harmonies and bluesy rhythm. Complimenting the goofy nature of the song, the coda is a tune hummed akin to Bobby McFerrin's 1988 hit Don't Worry, Be Happy.

Eternal Return is followed up Panpsych, a rather charming song I won't dissect. The previously stated themes continue to appear in such song and to restate such in different ways would be roundabout.

On the other hand, Spacesick is quite the catchy track. Another song about space and longing on the album but this time, unlike Lonely Cosmos, it is more lively. An emotion of longing for home - the ones that one leaves behind as they reach for the stars - lingers through the banger tunes. The sound still a mix of rock and country with grand horns and strings interspaced. The slices of lyrical reflection arouse those perplexing notes almost of a Bond theme song; bittersweet melodies summon zero gravity.

Afternoon country rock music on the radio - that is how I would describe Aerodynamic. The harmonies and melody speak of dismissal time and walking home from school and a house down the block is blasting radio music on full. This song may even be a song that could be the one blasted on such instance. I can't quite put it into words, but this song is quite familiar and, perhaps, some popular song may have become the inspiration for this - albeit I am not able to recall right now the one in question. Specifically, the instrumental of this may have been inspired by a certain guitar solo from a song that usually plays on the afternoon radio. Anyway, on the topic of instrumentals, it is certainly quite majestic - wonder felt in a few bars.

Sea of Doubt is quite the pop sounding song. Reminds me a lot of the artist Summer Salt especially with the catchy sections. The all-familiar chromatic descension is a weapon utilized to create a momentary "sea of doubt" in the harmonies. It manages to glue itself together with the other songs on the record despite less similarities with the other tracks.

The penultimate track, Silent Spirit, explores a rather different sound than the previous tracks - much more so than Sea of Doubt. Interesting melodies and chords are played on the strings and guitars especially on the solos.

Finally, we arrive at the final track, Grow Wings and Fly. The pentatonic intro immediately catches one's attention and the start of the drums can be thought of one beginning to fly. Strings evoke the feeling of soaring through the wind especially on the instrumental break after the second verse. This euphoric moment can be compared to the strings in the Super Smash Brothers Brawl remix of the track Stickerbrush Symphony. The ending verse: a climax of hope; catharsis achieved. The album ends on a quiet note thereafter.

Personally, I find this album to be very fun. The brass and catchy melodies and harmonies certainly caught my ear when I first heard these songs. Most of the songs I' saved to the playlists on the first listen. .Overall, I would rate this album a 10/10 and highly recommend it to anyone who likes fun goofy music that they can do a mini dance to every once in a while.

Despite all this yapping, I have not explored much of the venues of lyricism and symbolism in this album. This is due to two things: one, I prefer analyzing how a song feels over the meaning of its text (albeit there must always be some lyrical analysis present for it makes the song whole) and two, I do not want to analyze whatever is the lore of the Gizzverse - I am only in this for the banger music.

There was much joy that I found from exploring Phantom Island, and you should too!
